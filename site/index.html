Same production,
different shape

Arch Manning and Gunner Stockton produced almost exactly the same amount per dropback in 2025. How that production arrived could hardly have been more different — and the difference decides games.

Arch Manning · Texas Gunner Stockton · Georgia

The hypothesis was half right. Manning is measurably the boom-bust quarterback and Stockton the steady one — that shows up cleanly in the data. But the premium on brilliance does not exist where it was expected to be.

Manning's best plays are genuinely better: his 95th-percentile dropback is worth +0.52 EPA more than Stockton's. He pays roughly three times that back in bad snaps. In the highest-leverage quartile of 2025 he was −0.13 EPA per play while Stockton was +0.66. And across five seasons of real comeback attempts, having a fatter tail does not predict completing a comeback at all.

Where the two differ

SHIFT FUNCTION · 2025

This is the whole argument in one picture. Below the 70th percentile — the ordinary and bad snaps that make up most of a season — Stockton is better at every single quantile. At the 95th percentile the line crosses hard: Manning's elite play is the best thing either quarterback does.

That is the boom-bust signature, stated precisely. It is also why comparing averages tells you nothing here: the two means differ by 0.098 EPA per play, and this sample could only reliably detect a gap of 0.476.

What the shape costs

MEAN GAP DECOMPOSED

Manning wins the tail and loses everywhere else. The brilliance is real and it is worth +0.048 EPA per dropback. The floor costs him −0.100 and the routine middle another −0.046.

He is in the bad band on 45.1% of dropbacks against Stockton's 39.0%, and in the disaster band 11.3% against 8.9%. Put plainly: the highlight reel is paid for about three times over.

When it mattered most

LEVERAGE QUARTILES · 2025

This is the result that most contradicts the intuition. In the snaps that decided games, the ordering reverses and widens: Stockton +0.66, Manning −0.13.

It is not an artifact of who got more chances. A permutation test shuffles each quarterback's outcomes across his own situations, holding his opportunity mix exactly fixed. Stockton's leverage–outcome coupling comes back significant (p = 0.010); Manning's is indistinguishable from random (p = 0.958). Manning actually faced higher average leverage and still finished 2025 at −4.67 total win probability added against Stockton's +0.51.

Does brilliance win comebacks?

2,376 ATTEMPTS · 2022–2026

Rather than argue from one game, the hypothesis was moved somewhere it can actually be tested: every FBS team that trailed entering the fourth quarter since 2022 — 2,376 attempts, 156 completed comebacks.

Holding average passing efficiency constant, tail weight does not predict comebacks. The odds ratio is 0.85 with a 95% interval of 0.60–1.21, and the sign flips at a deeper deficit threshold. Average efficiency is the stronger term. The interval is tight enough that this is a genuine null rather than a shortage of data.

The Ohio State comeback

SEP 12, 2026 · TEXAS 24, OHIO STATE 23

Texas scored 21 unanswered. The comeback really begins with 5:38 left in the third quarter, where the model has them at 2.8%; by the start of the fourth, still trailing 23–3, they were at 4.7%. Two of those drives stayed alive because Manning converted 4th-and-9 and 4th-and-6. Both conversions look almost invisible on the curve above, and that is a property of the measuring stick rather than the plays.

Win probability barely moves when you are down three scores. From 20 points behind with 17 minutes left, the model gives Texas 8.6% — and a hypothetical 48-yard gain from there moves it to 8.7%. Nothing a quarterback does in that state registers, because the deficit, not the field position, is what is killing you. Only once the game re-enters the competitive band does a yard start to be worth win probability.

The 4th-and-6 was worth a real +0.032 — it multiplied Texas's chances by 1.27×. The 4th-and-9 was worth almost nothing in win-probability terms, for the counterintuitive reason that at 20 points down with a quarter to play, a punt and a turnover on downs are nearly the same outcome. Possession hardly matters when the scoreboard is the thing beating you.

Which is why this game needs two currencies, not one.

By EPA — which asks who moved the ball — Manning did 47% of the work on those three drives. By win probability — which asks who changed the odds — he did 32%. The gap is entirely about when each unit's value landed: Manning's came climbing out of the hole, where win probability is nearly flat; the run game's came at the goal line in the last five minutes, where it is steep.

Neither number is the honest one on its own. Manning moved the ball more than the win-probability split suggests, and the run game closed it out more than the EPA split suggests. What the game does not show is a quarterback winning it with explosive plays: his line was 23-of-37 for 196 yards at 5.3 yards an attempt with an interception, and his single largest win-probability play was worth +0.059. For scale, a 20-point fourth-quarter deficit was erased 6 times in 928 attempts across five seasons.

What this cannot tell you

LIMITS
  • Receiver drops are not in any free data. Zero occurrences across 375,000 plays — drops are a charting judgment only PFF and Sports Info Solutions record. Manning reportedly suffered 21 in 2025, fourth-most among SEC quarterbacks, with Ryan Wingo at an 11.5% drop rate. No equivalent figure exists for Georgia, so adjusting one side and not the other would simply manufacture a conclusion. Treat the drop story as real context for Manning that cannot be applied symmetrically.
  • Adjusted completion percentage could not be computed. Every component of its denominator — drops, throwaways, batted balls, spikes — is absent from the public feed.
  • Pressure is only a lower bound. The scorer-credited “QB hurried” tag fires on about 3% of plays and is credited inconsistently by venue (Manning 3.7%, Stockton 1.7% — almost certainly scorer variation, not a real 2× gap). PFF charts true pressure at roughly 35% of dropbacks.
  • Quarterback and team are collinear. Each played essentially all of his team's snaps, so nothing here cleanly separates the quarterback from his line, scheme and receivers. Texas's 2025 line allowed 23 sacks and pressure on close to 40% of dropbacks; its 2026 line grades among the best in the country.
  • One season of clutch is not a trait. Leverage residuals are famously unstable year to year. Stockton's p = 0.010 is one test among several run here and should be read as descriptive of 2025.
  • Two published data sources were unusable and were replaced. The bulk feed's win-probability columns are corrupted (routine plays swing win probability 30+ points on 5.8% of 2025 snaps and 12.7% of 2026 snaps); its EPA is sound and is used as shipped. Win probability here comes from a model fit for this study across 370,022 plays, calibrated out of fold to a Brier score of 0.154 against a 0.250 baseline.
